## Introduction: Beyond the Mother Road

Route 66 has a gravitational pull. Once you're on it, the rhythm of the road takes hold—the neon signs, the cracked asphalt of original alignment, the diners that haven't changed their menus since Eisenhower. It's easy to lock in and follow the white line all the way from Chicago to Santa Monica without ever turning the wheel more than a few degrees.

That's a mistake.

The Mother Road runs through some of the most geographically diverse terrain in the United States, and the best adventures often begin where the highway ends and the side roads start. Within a few hours' drive of nearly every major Route 66 town, there are canyons deeper than you'd expect, prairies that stretch to infinity, art colonies that defy explanation, and national parks that rank among the finest on Earth.

This guide is built for the Route 66 traveler who wants more. Not more miles for the sake of mileage, but more depth—the kind of depth that comes from parking the car at the rim of Palo Duro Canyon and watching the light change, or floating a canoe down Missouri's crystal-clear Ozark rivers, or standing in the surreal white gypsum dunes of White Sands as the sunset paints everything gold.

We've curated 25 detours, each one a standalone adventure ranging from a half-day escape to a three-day expedition. Every detour departs from a Route 66 town and loops back to the main corridor, so you never lose your place on the Mother Road. GPS coordinates are included for every major stop. Budget estimates keep your planning grounded. And each write-up is field-tested and editorially vetted—no recycled tourist-board copy, just genuine exploration guidance.

## Chapter 1: How to Plan Detours

### Timing Your Detours Within a Route 66 Trip

The fundamental question isn't whether to detour—it's when. A Route 66 through-drive typically takes 14 days at a comfortable pace. Adding detours means adding days, and every added day costs fuel, lodging, and food. The smartest approach: identify two or three must-do detours before you leave, build them into your itinerary, and leave room for one or two spontaneous side trips based on mood, weather, and energy levels.

Front-load detours if you're concerned about fatigue. The Illinois and Missouri detours happen early in your Route 66 journey when energy is high and the novelty factor is strong. By contrast, the Arizona detours—Grand Canyon, Sedona, Monument Valley—are natural breathers in the second week when the desert driving can feel relentless.

### Vehicle Considerations

Most detours in this guide are paved-road accessible in any standard sedan or SUV. Exceptions are noted: Monument Valley's scenic loop road is unpaved and rough, and some backcountry access roads in Death Valley require high clearance. If you're driving a rental, check your contract for unpaved-road restrictions.

Fuel planning is critical for desert detours. Fill your tank before leaving the Route 66 corridor, especially for Carlsbad Caverns, Marfa, White Sands, and Death Valley. Gas station spacing in southeastern New Mexico and the California desert can exceed 80 miles.

### Returning to Route 66

Every detour in this guide is designed as a loop or out-and-back that returns you to the Route 66 corridor. Departure and return cities are listed for each detour. In some cases—particularly the longer Texas and New Mexico detours—you'll rejoin Route 66 at a different town than where you left it, which means you may need to backtrack or skip a section. We've noted these situations explicitly.

### Budget Impact of Detours

Each detour adds costs beyond your base Route 66 budget. Expect:

- Additional fuel: $15–$60 per detour depending on distance
- Lodging: $60–$180 per night (budget to luxury range)
- Park entrance fees: $0–$35 per vehicle
- Food: $25–$65 per person per day
- Activities: $10–$50 per person (guided tours, boat rentals, etc.)

### How to Extend Your Trip: 14 Days to 21 or 28 Days

**21-Day Itinerary (14 base + 7 detour days)**
- Add 3–4 detours, choosing one per state cluster
- Recommended additions: Grand Canyon (2 days), Palo Duro Canyon (1 day), Santa Fe (2 days), Sedona (1 day), Joshua Tree (1 day)
- Additional budget: $800–$1,800 per person

**28-Day Itinerary (14 base + 14 detour days)**
- Add 6–8 detours for a comprehensive experience
- Recommended additions: Starved Rock (1 day), Ozarks (2 days), Wichita Mountains (1 day), Palo Duro Canyon (1 day), White Sands (2 days), Grand Canyon (2 days), Sedona (1 day), Monument Valley (2 days), Death Valley (2 days)
- Additional budget: $1,600–$3,500 per person

Detailed extended itineraries with day-by-day breakdowns are provided in Chapter 9.

**Morning: The Lakefront & Millennium Park**

Start at the official Route 66 Begin sign on Adams Street, then walk east toward Lake Michigan. Millennium Park (41.8826, -87.6226) is a masterclass in urban design—Cloud Gate ("The Bean") is iconic, but the Crown Fountain and Lurie Garden are worth lingering over. From here, walk north along the lakefront to Navy Pier (41.8919, -87.6051) for panoramic lake views and the Centennial Wheel.

**Afternoon: Architecture Boat Tour**

Board the Chicago Architecture Foundation Center River Cruise from the Chicago Riverwalk (41.8882, -87.6218). This 90-minute tour through the Chicago River system is consistently rated among the top tours in the world. You'll see the Marina City towers, Wrigley Building, Tribune Tower, and dozens of significant structures. Cost: $47–$55 per person. Book in advance during summer months.

**Evening: Deep-Dish Crawl**

- Lou Malnati's Pizzeria, 439 N Wells St (41.8904, -87.6338) — The butter crust. Order the Malnati Chicago Classic.
- Pequod's Pizza, 2207 N Clybourn Ave (41.9217, -87.6645) — Caramelized cheese crust that's almost burned. Almost.
- Giordano's, 730 N Rush St (41.8952, -87.6252) — Stuffed-style deep-dish, the tourist favorite that locals secretly enjoy.

### Detour 2: Starved Rock State Park

*Eighteen canyons carved by glacial meltwater, all within 90 minutes of Route 66.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Departure City** | Pontiac or Springfield, IL (Route 66) |

| **Drive Time** | 1.5 hours from Pontiac / 2.5 hours from Springfield |
| **Duration** | 1 full day |
| **Budget Estimate** | $30–$120 per person |

**Morning: Canyon Hiking**

Arrive early to beat the crowds—Starved Rock draws over two million visitors annually. The park (41.3198, -88.9937) features 18 canyons formed by glacial meltwater erosion along the Illinois River. Start with the Starved Rock Trail itself (1.5 miles round trip, easy) for river overlook views, then tackle the French Canyon and St. Louis Canyon trails for waterfall access. Spring is prime waterfall season; by August, many runs dry.

**Afternoon: Advanced Trails & River Views**

The Illinois Canyon Trail (moderate, 1 mile) rewards with a secluded canyon that sees fewer visitors. Serious hikers should tackle the full western trail system: Aurora Canyon, Sac Canyon, and Council Overhang offer 6+ miles of rugged terrain with bluff-top views of the Illinois River valley.

### Detour 3: Cahokia Mounds

*The largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico — and most Route 66 drivers have never heard of it.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Departure City** | Springfield, IL or approaching St. Louis (Route 66) |
| **Return City** | Same / continue to St. Louis |
| **Distance** | 95 miles from Springfield / 10 miles from East St. Louis |
| **Drive Time** | 1.5 hours from Springfield / 15 minutes from East St. Louis |
| **Duration** | Half day (3–4 hours) |
| **Budget Estimate** | $10–$30 per person |

Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site (38.6553, -90.0621) is a UNESCO World Heritage Site preserving the remains of the most sophisticated pre-Columbian civilization north of Mexico. At its peak around 1100 AD, Cahokia was home to 20,000 people—larger than London at the same period. The centerpiece is Monks Mound (38.6604, -90.0621), a 100-foot earthen pyramid that remains the largest pre-Columbian earthwork in the Americas.

Admission to the site is free; a suggested donation of $7 per adult supports maintenance. The Interpretive Center is open Wednesday through Sunday, 9 AM to 5 PM.

**Day 1: Current River & Big Spring**

The Ozark National Scenic Riverways (37.1397, -91.2571) protects 134 miles of the Current and Jacks Fork rivers in southeastern Missouri. Drive to Big Spring (36.9531, -91.0684), one of the largest springs in the U.S., pumping 286 million gallons daily into the Current River.

Rent canoes or kayaks at Alley Spring Canoe Rental near Eminence. A half-day float from Akers Ferry to Pulltite Spring (37.1186, -91.4033) covers 8 miles of gentle Class I water through limestone bluffs and hardwood forest. Cost: $40–$65 per canoe.

**Day 2 (Optional): Caves & Hiking**

Round Spring Cave (37.2747, -91.4114) offers ranger-led tours through a 350-million-year-old cavern system. Tours run Memorial Day through Labor Day, $8 per adult.

### Detour 21: Jerome Ghost Town

*A copper mining boom town that died, slid down a mountain, and was reborn as an art colony.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Departure City** | Flagstaff, AZ (Route 66) |
| **Return City** | Flagstaff, AZ (or Sedona loop) |
| **Distance** | 50 miles from Flagstaff |
| **Drive Time** | 1 hour from Flagstaff |
| **Duration** | 1 day (combine with Sedona for a full loop) |
| **Budget Estimate** | $30–$120 per person |

Jerome (34.7489, -112.1138) clings to Cleopatra Hill at 5,066 feet above the Verde Valley. Once Arizona's third-largest city with 15,000 residents, Jerome produced $800 million in copper, gold, and silver before the mines closed in 1953. Today, roughly 450 residents occupy the town's precarious streets.

The Jerome State Historic Park (34.7465, -112.1096), housed in the 1916 Douglas Mansion, documents the town's boom-bust-rebirth cycle. Admission: $7 adults.

## Conclusion

Route 66 is a line drawn across a continent. But the American West has never been about straight lines. It's about the canyon you didn't expect, the river that pulls you off-highway, the art installation in a town of 1,700 people that rewires how you see the desert.

These 25 detours are invitations, not obligations. Some—the Grand Canyon, White Sands, Monument Valley—are so extraordinary that skipping them feels like driving past the point of your own journey. Others—Cahokia Mounds, the Tallgrass Prairie, Jerome—are quieter revelations, the kind of places that embed themselves in memory and surface months later when someone asks what you remember most about the trip.

The Mother Road will always be there: the neon, the diners, the cracked pavement stretching toward the horizon. But the best road trips aren't measured in miles covered. They're measured in moments that alter your perspective. Turn the wheel. Take the side road. The detour is the destination.
