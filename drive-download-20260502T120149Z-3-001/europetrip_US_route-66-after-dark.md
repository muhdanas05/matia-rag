## Introduction: When the Sun Sets on Route 66

There is a moment, somewhere west of Amarillo and east of Tucumcari, when the last band of copper light dissolves into the horizon and the desert goes dark. Truly dark. The kind of darkness that modern America has largely engineered out of existence. And then—slowly, then all at once—the neon flickers on.

Route 66 is famous as a daytime road. The quirky motels, the crumbling gas stations, the Cadillac Ranch at golden hour. But the Mother Road has always been a nocturnal creature. In its heyday, the highway came alive after sunset: neon signs competed for the attention of bleary-eyed travelers, drive-in theaters projected double features against the desert sky, and 24-hour diners served coffee strong enough to fuel another hundred miles. The night was when Route 66 earned its keep.

This guide is dedicated to that other Route 66—the one that glows. We've cataloged more than 80 neon signs across eight states, each with GPS coordinates, optimal viewing times, and the stories behind the glass tubing. We've mapped every operational drive-in theater within striking distance of the route. We've identified the darkest skies along the corridor—places where the Milky Way arcs overhead with such clarity that you'll question whether you've ever actually seen the night sky before.

## Chapter 1: The Art of Route 66 Neon

### A Highway Written in Light

Neon and Route 66 are inseparable. By the 1930s, neon had migrated across the Atlantic and found its spiritual home along the highways of the West—none more so than Route 66. The Mother Road's 2,400-mile corridor became the longest neon gallery in the world, a luminous thread connecting Chicago's theater district to the Santa Monica Pier.

### The Golden Age: 1940–1965

The peak of Route 66 neon coincided with the peak of Route 66 itself. Between 1940 and 1965, the highway carried millions of travelers westward. Sign makers like Federal Electric, Young Electric Sign Company (YESCO), and local craftsmen produced signs of extraordinary complexity—animated arrows, cascading letters, figurative designs that depicted cowboys, swallows, wigwams, and stars.

### Decline and Rediscovery

The Interstate Highway Act of 1956 was a death sentence for thousands of Route 66 businesses, and their neon signs died with them. By the 1970s, plastic-faced fluorescent box signs had replaced most neon on American highways. The revival began in the 1990s, driven by a convergence of nostalgia, preservation activism, and the growing Route 66 tourism economy.

### Preservation Organizations

- **Route 66 Corridor Preservation Program (National Park Service)** — Federal cost-share grants (typically 50/50 matching) for restoration of historic Route 66 properties, including neon signage.
- **Neon Heritage Preservation Committee (NHPC)** — Responsible for restoring dozens of signs across Missouri and Illinois, including the Donut Drive-In, Munger Moss Motel, and Skylark Motel signs.
- **Route 66 Association of Missouri** — Partners with the City of St. Robert and Pulaski County Tourism Bureau on the Route 66 Neon Park in St. Robert, MO—the first outdoor neon exhibit of its kind on the Mother Road.
- **Historic Route 66 Association of Arizona** — Recently awarded $25,000 for three neon signs in Williams, AZ (Canyon Club, Sultana Bar, Turquoise Teepee).
- **National Trust for Historic Preservation** — Administers the Preserve Route 66 Legacy Business Grant Fund.
- **Neon Museum of St. Louis** — Open four days per month.

## Chapter 3: Drive-In Theaters Along Route 66

### The American Drive-In: A Cultural History

The drive-in theater is as American as Route 66 itself. Richard Hollingshead Jr. opened the first drive-in in Camden, New Jersey, in 1933. By 1958, there were over 4,000 drive-in theaters in the United States. Today, fewer than 300 drive-ins survive nationwide.

## Chapter 4: Dark Sky Destinations — Stargazing Along Route 66

### Why Route 66 Is a Stargazing Corridor

Route 66 crosses some of the darkest skies in the continental United States. Between Albuquerque and Flagstaff, the highway passes through vast stretches of desert and high plateau with minimal light pollution. On a clear October night in eastern Arizona, naked-eye visibility can exceed 7,000 stars.

### What to See in the Night Sky

**Milky Way Timing**

The Milky Way core is visible from late March through early October. Peak visibility occurs June through August. For Route 66 latitudes (34°–42°N), the core is best viewed looking south between 10 PM and 2 AM during summer months. October and November offer the last views of the core combined with the best atmospheric transparency—making fall the optimal season for combining Route 66 neon photography with Milky Way captures.

## Chapter 5: Night Photography Masterclass

### Neon Sign Photography

**Camera Settings for Neon**

| Setting | Recommended Value |
|---|---|
| ISO | 400–800 |
| Aperture | f/5.6–f/8 |
| Shutter Speed | 1/30s–1/4s (tripod required below 1/60s) |
| White Balance | Tungsten (3200K) or Auto |
| Metering | Spot meter on brightest tube, +1 stop |
| Format | RAW (essential) |

**Blue Hour vs. Full Dark**

The 30-minute window after sunset (blue hour) is the most productive time for neon photography. The sky retains enough ambient light to provide a rich navy-to-cobalt backdrop, and the neon tubes are fully visible. Full dark (90+ minutes after sunset) produces higher contrast—brilliant signs against pitch black. Bracket your exposures (±1 stop) and blend in post-processing.

**Tripod Recommendations**

- Budget: Neewer 77" Carbon Fiber ($80)
- Mid-range: MeFOTO RoadTrip ($180)
- Premium: Peak Design Travel Tripod ($350)

**Phone Photography Tips**

Modern smartphones (iPhone 15 Pro and later, Samsung Galaxy S24 and later, Google Pixel 8 and later) produce remarkable neon sign images. Key tips:
- Use Night Mode — hold the phone very steady for 3–5 seconds
- Tap to focus on the sign to lock exposure
- Use a phone tripod mount
- Avoid digital zoom — move physically closer
- Try ProRAW/Expert RAW for editing flexibility

**Composition for Neon Signs**

- **Include context:** The building, the road, the cars — don't isolate the sign from its environment
- **Leading lines:** Use the road, power lines, or building edges to draw the eye toward the sign
- **Reflections:** Wet pavement after rain doubles the neon. A spray bottle on a small foreground puddle works in dry conditions
- **Human scale:** A silhouetted figure beneath a sign adds narrative and scale

### Long Exposure Techniques

- **Car light trails:** 15–30 seconds at f/11, ISO 100
- **Star trails:** 20+ minute exposures (or stacked 30-second exposures) create circular star patterns
- **Neon reflections in water:** 2–8 second exposures smooth out ripples

### Light Painting Basics

Light painting uses a handheld light source during a long exposure to selectively illuminate subjects. Works brilliantly on Cadillac Ranch (illuminate individual cars with colored flashlights) and abandoned motels and gas stations.

Equipment needed: A strong flashlight (500+ lumens), colored gels ($10 variety pack), and a remote shutter release.

## Chapter 7: Desert Night Drives — Safe Routes & Breathtaking Experiences

### Recommended Night-Driving Segments

**Segment 1: Tucumcari to Santa Rosa, NM**
- Route: I-40 West | Distance: 59 miles | Duration: ~55 minutes

**Segment 2: Grants to Gallup, NM**

- GPS Start: (35.1475, -107.8512) | GPS End: (35.5287, -108.7426)

**Segment 3: Holbrook to Winslow, AZ**
- Route: I-40 West | Distance: 33 miles | Duration: ~30 minutes
- GPS Start: (34.9012, -110.1709) | GPS End: (35.0240, -110.6973)
- Caution: No services between the two towns. Ensure full tank before departure.

**Segment 4: Flagstaff to Williams, AZ**
- Route: I-40 West | Distance: 32 miles | Duration: ~30 minutes
- GPS Start: (35.1983, -111.6513) | GPS End: (35.2500, -112.1880)
- Caution: Elk frequently cross the highway between Flagstaff and Williams.

### Segments to AVOID at Night

- **Historic Route 66 between Seligman and Kingman, AZ** — No lighting, sharp curves, and steep grades through the Black Mountains. Drive this stretch in daylight.
- **Oatman Highway** — An extremely narrow, winding mountain road with no guardrails. Suicidal to drive at night.
- **I-40 through the Sandia Pass near Albuquerque** — Construction zones, merging traffic, and steep grades.
- **Any unpaved Route 66 alignment** — Crumbling pavement, no shoulders, no services. Daylight only.

### Wildlife Precautions

- **Deer and elk:** Most active at dusk and dawn. Use high beams when no oncoming traffic.
- **Pronghorn antelope:** Common in NM grasslands. Can appear suddenly and run alongside your vehicle.
- **Javelina (peccary):** Common in AZ desert areas. Usually travel in groups.
- **Coyotes:** Frequent road crossers. Large enough to damage a vehicle at speed.
- **Rattlesnakes:** Cross warm pavement at night. Watch where you step.

## Conclusion

Route 66 has always been two roads. The daytime road gets the postcards—the quirky attractions, the vintage gas pumps, the sweeping desert panoramas. But the nocturnal road is where the Mother Road reveals its soul. In the flicker of a hand-bent neon tube. In the silver arc of the Milky Way over a silent New Mexico pullout. In the warmth of a diner counter at 2 AM, where the coffee is strong and the waitress calls you "hon."

The neon signs you've cataloged in this guide are more than photo opportunities. They are the last visible evidence of a commercial ecosystem that defined American roadside culture. Every sign that still glows is a small act of defiance against the homogenizing force of the interstate highway system.

Drive this road after dark. Pull over at an unmarked spot in the Arizona high desert and look up. Visit a drive-in theater. Order a concrete at Ted Drewes at 10 PM on a July night. Let the neon guide you from town to town the way it was always meant to—as a series of glowing promises, each one kept.

The sun will rise, and Route 66 will become its daytime self again. But you'll know what it looks like in the dark. And that will change everything.
